# 27. AgentOps & Production Operations

An agent can work well on launch day and deteriorate later without an application-code change. A model alias may resolve differently, a policy document may become stale, a tool may change its output and traffic may shift toward cases the evaluation set barely covered. Operating the service means noticing those changes, limiting their impact and maintaining a clear recovery path. AgentOps is that continuing discipline, not a separate label that replaces ordinary service engineering.

This chapter connects quality objectives, releases, runtime controls, incidents and capacity. The operator needs to know what good service means, which release is running, how to stop harmful behaviour and how to recover obligations already created. ShopAssist provides the recurring example, while AegisOps supplies the higher-impact case of production operations. Both require oversight of behaviour as well as infrastructure health.

:::analogy An airline operating a changing fleet
An airline maintains service targets, crew checklists, controlled procedures, incident response and the ability to ground a particular operation. A new procedure is tested and introduced gradually rather than copied to every flight immediately. Agent operations similarly manage releases and live controls. Where it stops: a software flag is not instant everywhere, and grounding does not undo a flight already completed. Operators must verify propagation and separately reconcile effects that happened before containment.
:::

## What makes agent operations different

An ordinary service also has data-dependent behaviour, so avoid an absolute claim that only agents change without code. Agents amplify the issue because prompts, tool descriptions, model routes, memory and retrieved content can influence a sequence of decisions. Their costs and duration may vary substantially across requests. A response can be technically successful while factually wrong or falsely claiming an effect. Infrastructure availability alone therefore cannot establish service quality.

Treat the behavioural configuration as part of the release. Pin code, instruction packs, tool contracts, risk policy and supported model versions where possible. Record resolved model identities when a provider cannot offer immutable snapshots. Monitor knowledge and API changes that sit outside the release bundle. The operator should be able to answer which combination produced an affected run and what previous combination is known to be acceptable under current conditions.

Separate operational signals from their possible causes. More escalations may indicate a degraded model, stale knowledge, a safer policy or a change in customer needs. Lower latency may reflect useful optimisation or premature termination. Use traces, sampled evaluation and observed business outcomes to investigate. A dashboard colour is an invitation to a defined decision, not a substitute for understanding the denominator and population behind it.

## SLOs and SLIs for agents

A service-level indicator is a measurable aspect of service; an objective sets the target over a stated window and population. Task success should be based on checkable outcomes or calibrated evaluation, with critical slices reported separately. Availability measures whether requests are accepted and progressed according to the platform contract. Safe outcome distinguishes verified completion, appropriate escalation and bounded failure from an unsafe or unknown state. Do not count every status named completed as evidence of actual success.

Latency needs clear clocks. Time to first useful response, active processing time, end-to-end completion and approval wait answer different questions. A workflow with a thirty-minute human approval allowance cannot also promise that all end-to-end completions finish in forty-five seconds unless the populations differ. Define the applicable action class and whether waits are included. Resource objectives can include cost per successful task and recovery time, with units and aggregation rules stated.

An error budget converts a reliability objective into an allowed amount of observed bad service for the period. If availability target is 99.5%, the ordinary availability allowance is 0.5% of eligible events under the definition. Burn rate compares observed consumption with that allowance over relevant windows. Zero-tolerance observed unauthorised effects are different: there is no positive violation budget to spend or divide by. Page on such an event according to the incident policy rather than hiding it inside a tolerable average.

Do not declare dozens of objectives without owners and actionable consequences. Choose the indicators users and the business actually depend on, define data delay and sampling limits, and link each alert to a runbook. A quality objective from a small delayed sample cannot be read like an immediate unsampled API error counter. Use insufficient-evidence states and periodic human checks to avoid false precision in operating decisions.

## Runbooks, alerting and incident response

An alert should describe a condition and the first useful action. An observed unauthorised refund triggers containment of mutations and preservation of evidence. A success-rate burn directs the operator to recent model, prompt, policy and knowledge changes. A cost anomaly leads to loop counts, routing, cache use and spend reservations. Provider degradation points to breaker state and approved fallback capacity. Avoid an alert that merely says “agent unhappy” without a decision or owning team.

Incident handling begins with containment. Disable the affected tool or action class, reduce autonomy or route relevant tasks to human owners. Preserve the state, traces, audit and release configuration needed to understand the event under privacy policy. Determine affected tenants, action classes and versions. Then inspect actual effects, including outcome-unknown operations, before planning compensation. A rollback of configuration does not itself reverse a payment or deployment that already occurred.

Fix the relevant layer and verify it. A missing approval check needs a software correction; a stale policy source needs knowledge/version repair; an overloaded provider needs capacity or approved routing changes. Add reduced regression cases and update the threat model when appropriate. The postmortem should connect the control failure, contributing conditions and demonstrated repair rather than declaring that a new prompt will prevent every recurrence.

The following runbook outline is deliberately a template. Replace placeholders with real owners, dashboards, policies and verified procedures before operational use. Its value is ordering: stop new harm, preserve evidence, identify effects and recover them. Keep the containment action available independently of the model whose behaviour is under investigation. Test the outline in a game day instead of discovering permissions or missing contacts during a live incident.

## Rollouts for prompts, policies, models and configuration

Every behaviour-changing artifact needs versioning and release ownership. Review tool/schema changes, prompt packs, routing and autonomy settings with the relevant domain owner. A release manifest should let the runtime pin the combination used by a run. Development, staging and production promotion use software tests and behavioural gates. A model upgrade can change tool selection or refusal patterns even when the application's interface remains compatible, so evaluate it as a behaviour change.

Keep rollback targets usable. The previous release may depend on a removed tool version or stale knowledge, so “previous” is not automatically known-good forever. Preserve compatible dependencies and test the supported rollback window. Decide whether in-flight runs retain their pinned version, pause or migrate through an explicit state-compatible procedure. Current emergency restrictions must still apply at execution boundaries, even if ordinary prompts and model configuration remain pinned for reproducibility.

## Shadow, canary and staged release

A shadow release runs the candidate on a controlled copy of live input without delivering its effects to users. Stub mutations and apply the same privacy and resource rules to the copied data. Read-only shadow calls can still cost money or disclose data, so their scope matters. Compare candidate outputs and trajectories with the baseline, but remember that a shadow environment may not reproduce state changes or feedback from actual users.

A canary release sends a bounded portion of real eligible traffic to the candidate. Randomise or use an approved sticky allocation that prevents a session from changing versions unexpectedly. Include tenant and session context in the allocation identity where appropriate, and keep risk/region constraints ahead of the traffic choice. The following pure example uses an integer share in basis points. Allocation controls exposure; it does not establish that enough observations exist to promote the release.

```python
import hashlib

def choose_release(tenant, session, stable, candidate, share):
    if type(share) is not int or not 0 <= share <= 10000:
        raise ValueError("share must be integer basis points")
    identity = f"{tenant}|{session}".encode()
    bucket = int.from_bytes(
        hashlib.sha256(identity).digest()[:8], "big") % 10000
    return candidate if bucket < share else stable
```

Promotion needs a declared measurement policy. Report candidate and baseline counts, time window, slice definitions and uncertainty for their difference. The candidate's own lower bound compared with a baseline point estimate ignores baseline uncertainty. Overlapping marginal intervals alone do not prove no regression. For live traffic, use an appropriate two-sample or planned experimental method; offline paired cases use the methods in Chapter 25. Plan sequential checks and multiple critical slices rather than inspecting continuously until a favourable result appears.

Staging by risk can start with lower-impact intents or selected tenants before expanding. Keep the selection explicit because results from that population may not generalise to high-risk traffic. A two-percent sample of a five-percent canary can be too small to detect a quality change quickly. State observation counts and data delay, not just percentages. An observed forbidden effect can trigger immediate containment while slower quality estimates accumulate; those are different decision mechanisms.

## Feature flags and autonomy controls

Flags can enable an agent, restrict a tool, disable mutations, select approved model routes or tighten spend limits. Evaluate them at the relevant runtime boundaries, especially immediately before a consequential execution. A model's offered tool list may be stale by the time it requests an action. The executor must consult the current emergency policy rather than trusting the earlier advertisement. Audit changes with owner, version, scope and time.

Define behaviour when the flag service is unavailable or local cached policy is too old. Mutations can fail closed while approved reads and handovers remain useful. A flag that defaults to allow under an outage is a different risk choice and requires explicit justification. Measure propagation and verify that the control reaches all workers and tool gateways. “Instant kill switch” is an operational target, not a property guaranteed by setting one value in a configuration store.

Use narrower controls where they contain the issue without unnecessary disruption. A tool restriction, agent-action restriction, tenant route and global disable are overlapping scopes, not necessarily one strict hierarchy. Show which requests and in-flight operations each control affects. Emergency changes should remain usable during the provider or agent failure being contained and should not require a fresh model call to decide whether to apply them.

## Online quality monitoring and drift

Monitor sampled outcomes by critical slice together with behaviour and business signals. Tool mix, repeated searches, escalation and refusal patterns can reveal a change before average success moves. Input drift identifies new languages, intents or resource conditions. Retrieval health includes source freshness, empty results and applicable evidence, not only index uptime. Calibrate and version online judges so a grader change is not mistaken for an agent regression.

Quality monitoring can also reveal gradual decline. A provider alias changes, a knowledge source grows obsolete or a downstream API adds a field the adapter misinterprets. Retain provenance to compare successful and failing runs. A green infrastructure dashboard does not rule out any of these conditions. Connect the monitor to a defined rollback, restriction or investigation policy rather than expecting operators to notice a small chart shift manually.

## Rollback and kill switches

Rollback restores a supported previous behaviour bundle for new or explicitly managed runs. A kill switch stops a class of work now, according to its propagation and execution-boundary contract. Neither automatically undoes earlier side effects. Record pending and outcome-unknown operations before choosing compensation, and check current resource state. Some effects are irreversible or require business-owner handling, so a disabled tool is not equivalent to a fully recovered incident.

Rehearse both controls. Launch representative traffic, engage the mutation restriction and verify zero new protected effects after the documented enforcement point. Confirm that diagnosis and escalation still work where allowed. Roll back a candidate and check release identities on new requests and in-flight records. Measure actual delay and failure behaviour. A runbook that says one click without proving what changed leaves an important uncertainty for the live incident.

## Capacity and dependency quotas

Provider quotas, tool limits, state storage and human queues all constrain capacity. Allocate requests, tokens and concurrency across agents and tenants, with priorities and headroom for recovery. A runaway low-priority workflow should not starve interactive tasks or incident responders. Forecast using traffic, steps and token-length distributions rather than one average call. Load-test non-model components with fakes and validate model/provider capacity under the approved environment.

Plan failover capacity too. A fallback provider that meets the quality bar but has no spare quota cannot absorb an outage. A human queue that receives every task after global disable needs its own staffing and latency plan. Backpressure and admission control should defer or refuse work clearly when capacity is unavailable. Scaling workers indefinitely can worsen the bottleneck by increasing pressure on the same shared model or database limit.

## Build it: operate one release end to end

Choose one ShopAssist behaviour change and write its manifest, objectives, canary allocation, analysis rule, emergency restrictions and incident runbook. Run controlled shadow traffic, then a bounded eligible canary under the declared gates. Inject a quality regression and a forbidden-effect probe. Verify that the appropriate control responds, that the evidence remains inspectable and that recovery handles existing effects separately. The deliverable is an operated, reviewable process rather than an attractive release-flow picture.
