# 17. Multi-Agent Architectures

ShopAssist must answer a customer's question about a delayed refund. The answer needs payment records, policy evidence and a customer-facing explanation. It is tempting to create three agents because there are three nouns in that description. That is not yet a reason to use a team. One agent with well-designed read tools may complete the work more cheaply and with fewer opportunities to lose context. The architecture needs a measurable benefit, not an appealing organisation chart.

A multi-agent system divides work between independently instructed components. The division can create specialisation, parallelism or a useful permission boundary. It also adds delegation decisions, copied context, latency and failure paths. This chapter shows how to choose that division, keep one owner of the overall outcome, exchange scoped information and measure whether the team improves on a simpler baseline. Multiple agents are an engineering option rather than an automatic level of sophistication.

:::analogy A project team with one accountable manager
A manager assigns a policy researcher and a payment analyst different tasks, gives each the relevant documents and combines their reports. The manager owns the deadline and decides what is ready to deliver. Specialists help when their separate skills or access matter; meetings and repeated explanations can otherwise consume the benefit. Where it stops: agents do not have human professional judgement or independent expertise merely because they have job titles. Their errors may be correlated, and software must enforce ownership and permissions.
:::

## When multiple agents are justified

Start by identifying the obstacle a single agent cannot handle well. A narrow refund specialist may need different policy and tools from a shipping specialist. Separate workers can each read a large corpus slice that would not fit in one context. Independent investigations can run in parallel. Another team may own a remote capability that the caller should not absorb. A read-only investigator and an approved action executor can also have intentionally different authority.

Those benefits differ. Specialisation improves the match between instruction, tools and task. Context partitioning limits what each worker sees. Parallelism changes elapsed time. Isolation changes worst-case harm. An ownership boundary preserves another team's operational responsibilities. Do not bundle them into an unmeasured claim that “a team is smarter.” Identify which benefit you expect, how you will measure it and what coordination cost is acceptable for that benefit.

For a straightforward refund-status question, ShopAssist can call an order tool and a payment tool in one controlled run. A team becomes more plausible when the case involves a disputed policy exception, several payment systems or separate data owners. Even then, compare the team with a single agent and a deterministic workflow on the same representative cases. Measure correct outcomes, forbidden actions, elapsed time and cost. A longer answer or a larger number of agents is not evidence of improvement.

Least privilege means restricting each worker to the capabilities and information required for its task. That permission argument also requires care. Giving a research worker only read tools reduces its authority, but a supervisor with unrestricted write tools can still act on a manipulated research report. The boundary is useful when reports are treated as untrusted evidence and the sensitive executor applies independent policy. Workers should not manufacture an approval or convert their confidence into authorisation. Separation is valuable because it makes enforced responsibility clearer, not because crossing an agent boundary cleanses data.

## Core patterns and their ownership rules

In supervisor-worker coordination, the supervisor owns the goal, plan and final stopping decision. It assigns bounded tasks and integrates worker results. This fits AegisOps investigations where metrics, logs and changes require different evidence collection. The supervisor can become a bottleneck, so delegation must earn its cost. It need not use a model for every assignment: deterministic dispatch can handle known stages, while a model helps with genuinely uncertain choices.

A router-specialist design selects one destination and hands the request to that specialist. There is little ongoing coordination. ShopAssist can route an order-status question to a status workflow and a refund dispute to a refunds agent. The router needs a clear fallback when intent is ambiguous, and the receiving specialist must be able to reject a misrouted request. An initial classification is not a permanent entitlement to every tool in the selected domain.

A handoff chain changes the owner of the next conversational phase. Triage may transfer to refunds, then to a human. Record who owns the continuation and what unresolved obligations are transferred. Handoffs become unstable when overlapping criteria allow each agent to send the same request back. The system needs phase ownership, a bounded handoff count and escalation for repeated transfers. A helpful explanation of responsibilities in prompts is useful, but it is weaker than an enforced transition rule.

Hierarchy introduces supervisors of supervisors. It can mirror large tasks or independently owned departments, but every level adds information compression and decision delay. A leaf worker's qualified finding can become an unqualified conclusion after several summaries. Keep hierarchies shallow unless the task size or ownership structure requires depth. Preserve evidence references and uncertainty through each level, and make the root owner responsible for the whole run's budget and termination.

Choose a pattern by the control relationship rather than by drawing preference. A supervisor retains the overall task. A router selects a path. A handoff transfers the next phase. A hierarchy groups delegated work. These can coexist, but the implementation must say which component may finish the run at each point. Otherwise several agents can emit incompatible final answers, or every agent can wait for another to decide that enough work has been done.

## Shared state versus message passing

Shared state resembles the manager's project record. It gives a central view of the goal, evidence and outstanding work. If every worker can edit every field, however, one worker may overwrite another's findings or change a budget it should only consume. Shared visibility is not shared ownership. The record needs field ownership and merge rules, and each worker should read only the portion its task requires.

Message passing gives each worker a bounded brief and collects a response. This makes information flow explicit and reduces unnecessary context, but it can hide useful facts from a worker unless the owner sends them. A strong hybrid keeps canonical state at the supervisor while workers receive scoped envelopes and return structured updates. The supervisor validates and merges those updates. Workers do not directly change the overall outcome, approval status or global budget.

A delegation envelope should describe the requested finding, the relevant context slice, evidence references, allowed tools, deadline and output contract. Tool restrictions must be enforced by the worker runtime, not merely written into a field. The result should distinguish done, partial, failed and needs-input, and should include provenance, remaining uncertainty and actual resource usage. A result containing suggestions to modify another worker's instructions remains data for the owner to evaluate.

The following data contracts illustrate that boundary. They describe a report rather than an execution capability. The surrounding worker service must authenticate the caller, validate evidence access and enforce the tool and budget policy. A production model would use more specific finding and proposal schemas; unrestricted dictionaries are intentionally absent here because the point is to make the report's authority and status visible.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Literal

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class WorkOrder:
    operation_id: str
    task: str
    evidence_ids: tuple[str, ...]
    allowed_tools: tuple[str, ...]
    deadline_at: str
    max_calls: int

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Finding:
    claim: str
    evidence_ids: tuple[str, ...]
    limitation: str | None

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class WorkerReport:
    status: Literal["done", "partial", "failed", "needs_input"]
    findings: tuple[Finding, ...]
    spent_usd: float
    reason: str | None
```

## Build it: supervisor with research, data and action workers

Consider a duplicate-charge dispute. The supervisor first asks the payment analyst to identify captures for the customer's order and asks the policy researcher for the relevant refund rule. Each receives the order scope and necessary evidence access; neither receives unrelated customer history. The analyst reports two captures with distinct payment IDs. The researcher reports the conditions for refunding a duplicate capture, including any exclusions. Those are evidence inputs, not yet a financial decision.

The supervisor checks whether the findings refer to the same order, tenant and time window. If the payment report is partial or the policy version is outdated, it requests the missing evidence or escalates. When the facts support a refund, the action worker builds a proposal containing one payment ID, amount, currency, reason, evidence IDs and current preconditions. It has no permission to issue the refund. A human or pre-approved policy can authorise the exact proposal under Chapter 18's rules.

Only the protected executor crosses the financial boundary. It validates the recorded approval, checks the resource state and uses a stable operation identity. The supervisor then verifies the observed payment outcome and prepares the reply. A customer-facing answer should distinguish proposed, approved, submitted and verified refund states. A team that turns “proposal drafted” into “money returned” has lost an essential distinction, even if each individual worker returned schema-valid output.

This workflow can use deterministic dispatch for the two known reads and proposal preparation. A model-driven supervisor adds value when deciding which further investigation would resolve a conflicting finding. Its delegation choices still require validation against a worker registry and the current task budget. Unknown worker names, unauthorised tools or repeated identical briefs are controlled errors. A central owner makes those controls easier to inspect than allowing workers to recruit arbitrary helpers without limits.

## Parallel specialists and ensembles

Parallel specialists investigate different aspects of one problem. AegisOps can gather metrics, logs and recent changes concurrently, then compare whether the observations support the same timeline. This saves elapsed time when branches are independent and capacity is available. Their outputs should meet through a deadline-aware join, which records missing branches and conflicts. More concurrent workers do not help if all of them contend for one rate-limited search endpoint.

An ensemble asks several workers to solve the same problem. It can reveal disagreement, but agreement is not a proof of correctness. Workers using the same model, prompt and evidence can share a false premise. For a high-impact decision, independent evidence collection or an external outcome check is often more valuable than another opinion based on the same context. Measure error correlation and the reliability of the combination rule rather than assuming several votes multiply confidence.

The combining rule must match the task. Majority vote may fit a well-defined classification, while a judge can compare open-ended proposals against a rubric. Both can fail when all candidates are wrong or the judge lacks the evidence needed to identify the error. An escalation-on-disagreement policy may be appropriate when the cost of a wrong action is high. Define the operational cost of that policy too: excessive disagreement can overwhelm the human queue.

Use ensembles selectively. A routine order-status query rarely justifies several independent model answers. An ambiguous incident hypothesis may justify a second investigation with a different evidence source. Compare quality improvement with calls, tokens, latency and human review demand. A worker's role name, model family or different random seed does not by itself establish meaningful independence. The data and checking method matter as much as the number of participants.

## Coordination overhead, duplicate work and loop detection

Coordination creates work that does not directly advance the user's goal. The supervisor describes tasks, workers summarise context and the supervisor integrates reports. Some of this is necessary. It becomes waste when the team repeatedly restates the same brief, searches the same records or asks another agent to make a decision the caller could make deterministically. A delegation does not universally require two model calls, but every delegation adds some transport, bookkeeping and context-handling cost.

Record delegation identities and the evidence already collected. If two workers need the same payment record, fetch it once through an authorised shared evidence service rather than making each rediscover it. A stable task fingerprint can detect unchanged repeated work, but it should include relevant resource versions and inputs. Re-running a task after new evidence arrives may be legitimate; blocking solely because the natural-language task sounds similar can suppress useful investigation.

Ping-pong is a control failure: worker A redirects to B, and B redirects to A. Loop detection looks for repeated ownership transitions or repeated work without changed evidence. Bound handoffs, keep a history of phase ownership and identify a fallback owner. The same principle applies to supervisors that repeatedly commission reports without making progress. Track new evidence, changed hypotheses or completed obligations. When those measures remain stagnant, stop with an explanation and escalation rather than merely spending the next round budget.

Context fragmentation is another team failure. A worker may not know that another worker ruled out a crucial hypothesis. The supervisor should send relevant evidence references when reassigning work, while preserving access controls and uncertainty. Summaries help but can lose qualifications. Keep the source records available for verification and make a report's limitations explicit. A confident synthesis that erased all workers' caveats is worse than an honestly partial result.

## Provenance and conflicting writes

Subagent reports are data, including any instructions copied from documents, web pages or tool output. They cannot override the user's authorised goal or the platform policy. The owner validates structure and resource scope, retains provenance and treats suggestions as proposals. This rule must also apply to file-based reports and shared memory; a worker cannot gain control merely by writing an instruction into a location another agent will read.

Partition conflicting work before launching parallel agents. For repository work, assign disjoint files or make one worker own a shared file. For operational changes, serialise actions on the same service or resource through the protected executor. Reducers can merge findings, but they do not resolve two incompatible external mutations. Locks or queues also need the ownership and recovery contract described in Chapter 29; a label saying “shared state” is not a concurrency mechanism.

## Termination, ownership and accountability

The overall owner must decide when the goal is met, impossible within scope or ready for a human. Workers can report completion of their subtask, not silently finish the entire run. Global budgets belong to the owner, with smaller allocations to workers. A worker must not reset its allowance by delegating again, and the parent must account for remote usage where the contract supports it. Missing cost information is a limitation to manage, not permission for unbounded calls.

Human accountability remains with the team operating the system. An audit trail should show the requested goal, the assigned briefs, worker identities and versions, evidence returned, proposals and final effects. This supports incident review and helps decide whether a team design should be simplified. If the multi-agent system does not improve a meaningful constraint over the baseline, collapsing it is an engineering improvement rather than a loss of sophistication.
